How to track customer outstanding and collect payments faster

8 min read · Updated 27 Sep 2026

Short answer: record every credit sale as a dated bill against the customer, record every payment — including partial payments — against a specific bill, and review the unpaid list oldest-first every week. The number that matters is not total outstanding but who owes what, since when. Collections get faster when that list is current, shared with whoever does the chasing, and visible to the customer too.

Why udhaar tracking breaks as you grow

Almost every wholesaler and distributor in India sells on credit. Fifteen days, thirty days, “next month when my payment comes”. When you have twenty regular parties and you personally handle all of them, memory and a register work. The trouble starts when the business grows and the owner is no longer the only one giving credit or collecting money.

The questions sound like this:

  • “Kitna baaki hai?” — and three people give three different numbers.
  • “He says he paid last week. Did we get it? Against which bill?”
  • “We dispatched another load to him — didn’t anyone check he still owes from June?”
  • “The accountant’s report says one thing, the salesman’s notebook says another.”

Underneath those questions are a few very specific failures:

  • Credit is given in one place and recorded in another. The order is confirmed on WhatsApp, the bill is made later, and sometimes the bill is never made.
  • Partial payments are recorded as lump sums. “Received ₹40,000 from Sharma Traders” goes into the account, but nobody notes which bills it cleared. After a few months you know the balance but not how old it is.
  • Payments arrive through too many channels. UPI, NEFT, cheque, cash to the delivery boy. Each lands in a different place, and matching them to bills becomes a monthly headache instead of a daily habit.
  • The books are updated late. Many businesses post entries in their accounting software weekly or monthly. That is fine for GST and accounts, but the person deciding whether to send the next dispatch today is looking at last month’s picture.
  • Nobody owns the follow-up. Everyone knows a party is slow. Nobody has been told to call him on Tuesday and write down what he promised.

The result is not one big loss. It is working capital stuck with customers, a few dues that quietly go bad each year, and an owner who spends evenings reconciling instead of selling.

Steps that work even without new software

You can fix most of this with discipline and a spreadsheet, as long as the order and payment volume is manageable. These steps are the same whether you use a register, Excel or a proper system — software only makes them easier to keep up.

  1. Clean up the customer list. One record per customer, one spelling, one contact, and the agreed credit terms written down. “Sharma Traders”, “Sharma Trdrs” and “Sharma ji (Karol Bagh)” must become one party, or their dues will never add up.
  2. Record every credit sale as a dated bill. Each sale gets a bill number, bill date, amount and due date. The due date comes from the customer’s terms: bill date plus 30 days, for example. No bill, no dispatch.
  3. Record payments against specific bills. When ₹40,000 comes in, apply it to named bills — normally the oldest first — and note the mode and reference (UTR number, cheque number, or who collected the cash). A partial payment reduces that bill’s balance; the rest of the bill keeps its original date.
  4. Age every open bill. In Excel, a column with =TODAY()-BillDate gives days outstanding. Group into 0–30, 31–60, 61–90 and 90+ days. A customer owing ₹2 lakh for 20 days is a very different problem from one owing ₹2 lakh for 110 days.
  5. Run a weekly collection review. Same day every week, fifteen minutes. Sort by age, then by amount. For each serious due, assign one person to follow up and write down the promised date. Next week, start with the promises that were missed.
  6. Set a credit hold rule in advance. Decide, as the owner, when new dispatches stop: over a credit limit, or once any bill crosses 60 days. The salesperson in front of a customer should not be making that call on the spot.
  7. Send bill-wise statements and reconcile. Share a statement listing each open bill, not just a total. Disputes (“that bill was returned goods”, “we paid that by cheque”) surface in week two instead of month six. Match recorded payments to the bank statement every week.

If you do only one thing from this list, do step 3. Applying payments to bills is what makes ageing, statements and follow-ups possible at all.

Comparing your options honestly

There is no single right tool. What fits depends on how many credit customers you have, how many people take orders and collect money, and how quickly you need the picture to be current.

OptionWorks well whenWhere it breaks for dues
Notebook or khata registerOne person handles all credit and a few dozen partiesNo ageing, no sharing, lost if the book is lost; depends on one person’s handwriting and memory
Excel or Google SheetsOne disciplined owner maintains it; low order volumeSeveral editors create conflicting versions; payments rarely get applied bill-wise; stock and orders live in separate files
Mobile billing and khata appsSmall shops and traders who want quick credit entries and reminders on a phoneUsually built around one user or counter; may not connect dues to your order workflow, stock and suppliers; data sits with the app provider
Accounting software such as TallyBooks, GST and statutory reporting, handled by an accountantOutstanding is only as current as the last posting; sales and dispatch teams often do not work in it day to day
Operations system such as BOOMMultiple staff take orders, dispatch and collect; you want dues tied to orders in real timeMore setup than a sheet; a one-time investment that only makes sense at a certain scale

Many businesses end up with two tools: accounting software for the books, and something else for daily operations. The mistake is when the “something else” is five WhatsApp groups and a personal notebook.

Frequently asked questions

What is the simplest way to track customer outstanding?

Record every credit sale as a dated bill against the customer, record every payment against a specific bill, and review the unpaid list oldest-first once a week. A well-kept spreadsheet can do this for a small number of customers; it breaks when several people take orders and collect money.

How should partial payments be recorded?

Apply the partial payment to a specific bill, usually the oldest one, and carry the remaining balance on that bill with its original date. Recording it as a lump sum against the customer hides how old the remaining due really is.

What is receivables ageing?

Ageing groups unpaid bills by how long they have been outstanding, usually 0-30, 31-60, 61-90 and 90+ days. Two customers can owe the same amount, but the one whose money is 90 days old is the bigger risk and should be chased first.

Does BOOM replace Tally for customer ledgers?

No. BOOM is the operations layer where orders, bills, dues and payments are recorded day to day. Your accountant keeps using Tally or other accounting software for books, GST and statutory work. BOOM data can be exported for them.
