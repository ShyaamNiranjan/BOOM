How to find your real profit per product, customer and order

9 min read · Updated 27 Sep 2026

Short answer: real profit per product is the net selling price after every discount and scheme, minus the landed cost — purchase price plus freight, handling, packing and duty — not the purchase invoice price. Calculate it per sale line, then total it by product, by customer and by order, and add the cost of credit and delivery for each customer. Most margin leakage hides in landed cost nobody allocated and discounts nobody recorded.

Why “sales are up but the money isn’t”

This is one of the most common things owners of trading and distribution businesses say: turnover grew, the godown is busy, and yet the bank balance does not reflect it. Ask what the margin is and the answer is usually a single number — “we make around 12% on everything”. That number was true once, for some products, for some customers.

Here is where it quietly stops being true:

  • Purchase price changed, selling price did not. The supplier raised rates twice this year. The price list your salesmen quote was last updated before both increases.
  • Freight and handling are treated as overheads. The transporter is paid in cash or at month-end and booked as a general expense. It never gets added to the cost of the goods it carried, so heavy, low-value products look far more profitable than they are.
  • Discounts are given verbally. An extra 2% for a big party, a free carton on 20, rounding off the bill. None of it lands in a sheet that anyone analyses.
  • Returns, damages and short supply. Broken cartons, expired stock and goods sent back rarely get charged against the product or customer that caused them.
  • Credit has a cost. A customer who pays in 90 days is using your money for 90 days. If you run on a CC limit or borrowed funds, that interest is part of what that customer costs you.
  • Markup and margin get mixed up. Buying at ₹100 and selling at ₹120 is a 20% markup but only a 16.7% margin. Plan with the wrong one and every estimate is too optimistic.

Your accountant’s profit and loss statement will be correct for the business as a whole. What it usually cannot tell you is which products, customers and orders produced that profit, and which ones ate into it.

A worked example

An illustrative example (the numbers are made up, the pattern is not). A distributor buys a carton of a product at ₹800. Inward freight works out to ₹40 a carton and loading, unloading and godown handling to ₹10. Landed cost: ₹850, not ₹800.

  • Customer A pays list price of ₹950 within 15 days. Margin: ₹100 a carton, 10.5%.
  • Customer B is a large account with a 5% discount, so pays ₹902.50, on 75-day credit. Margin before credit cost: ₹52.50, or 5.8%. At 12% a year, carrying ₹902.50 for 75 days costs about ₹22. Real margin: roughly ₹30 a carton, about 3.4%.

Measured against the purchase invoice alone, Customer A looks like ₹150 a carton and Customer B like ₹102.50. In reality, the big customer everyone is proud of earns less than a third of what the smaller one does. Add a free delivery and one damaged carton a month, and that account may be losing money.

Steps that work even without new software

You can do this in Excel if you are consistent. Start with your top 20 products and top 20 customers; that is usually most of the turnover.

  1. Calculate landed cost per unit. For each purchase, add purchase price, inward freight, loading and unloading, packing, insurance and, for imports, customs duty and clearing and forwarding charges. Divide by units actually received in good condition. When one freight bill covers several products, split it by weight, volume or value — pick one rule and use it every time.
  2. Pick one costing method. Either a weighted average of landed cost across purchases, or the latest landed cost. Weighted average is steadier; latest cost warns you sooner when rates rise. Mixing both makes the numbers meaningless.
  3. Record the net selling price on every line. After trade discount, cash discount, schemes and free quantity. If 20 cartons were billed and 1 was free, the price per carton is the bill amount divided by 21.
  4. Calculate margin per line. Margin = (net selling price − landed cost) × quantity. Margin % = margin ÷ net revenue. Keep it at line level; averages hide the problem.
  5. Roll up by product, customer and order. A pivot table on the line data gives you all three. Sort from lowest margin to highest. The bottom of each list is where your attention should go.
  6. Add the cost of serving each customer. Delivery you pay for, and credit cost: amount outstanding × your interest rate × days ÷ 365. This is where profitable-looking accounts often turn thin.
  7. Review monthly and act. Reprice products where cost moved, renegotiate discounts or credit days with thin-margin customers, change suppliers where landed cost is out of line, or stop pushing products that do not earn.

The hard part is not the formula. It is getting purchase costs, freight and discounts recorded consistently by several people, every day, in a form you can analyse.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate profit per product in a wholesale business?

Take the net selling price after all discounts and schemes, subtract the landed cost per unit, and multiply by quantity sold. Do it per sale line, then total by product. Using the purchase invoice price instead of landed cost overstates margin.

What is landed cost?

Landed cost is the full cost of getting a product into your godown ready to sell: purchase price plus inward freight, loading and unloading, packing, insurance and, for imports, customs duty and clearing charges, spread over the units received in good condition.

What is the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is profit as a percentage of cost; margin is profit as a percentage of selling price. A product bought at ₹100 and sold at ₹120 has a 20% markup but only a 16.7% margin. Mixing the two is a common reason businesses think they earn more than they do.
