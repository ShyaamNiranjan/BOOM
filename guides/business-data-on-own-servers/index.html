How to keep your business data on your own servers (self-hosted ERP)

9 min read · Updated 27 Sep 2026

Short answer: run your business software and its database on a server your company controls — an office server, a VPS or a private cloud account registered in the company’s name — instead of on a vendor’s shared cloud. That gives you control over where the data lives, who can reach it, and whether you keep running regardless of any vendor’s pricing or future. In exchange, you own the responsibilities: the server, backups you actually test, updates, access control, and a named person accountable for them.

Why owners start asking where their data lives

For a wholesaler, distributor or manufacturer, the operational data is the business: every customer, the rate each one gets, who owes what, which suppliers you depend on, what sells and at what margin. It usually takes years to build. The questions owners ask when they think about moving it into software are very practical:

  • “If this app company raises prices next year, what do I do?”
  • “If I stop paying, can I still see my own orders and dues?”
  • “Can I take all my data out, in a form I can actually open?”
  • “Every new staff member adds a per-user fee. Why am I paying rent on my own team?”
  • “Who else can see my customer list and my rates?”

These are fair questions, and they are not an argument that cloud software is bad. Subscription software works well for many businesses. But it helps to be clear about what lock-in looks like:

  • Pricing you do not control. Plans, per-seat rates and feature tiers can change after your business depends on the tool.
  • Access that depends on payment. If the subscription lapses or the account is suspended, access to your history can stop with it.
  • Exports that are partial. Some tools let you download reports but not the complete, linked records you would need to move elsewhere.
  • Customization limits. Shared platforms serve many customers with one product, so your particular workflow may never fit.
  • Vendor continuity. Products are discontinued, merged or acquired. Your operations should not depend on that.

What self-hosting actually requires

Self-hosting is not free and it is not magic. It moves responsibility from a vendor to you. Before choosing it, be honest about whether your business can carry these, either in-house or through an IT partner.

  1. Choose where the server lives. An on-premise server in your office needs reliable power with UPS backup, a stable internet line if anyone works remotely, and a physically secure spot. A VPS from a hosting provider removes the hardware worry but has its own monthly or yearly hosting bill — an infrastructure cost, not a software subscription. A private cloud instance suits businesses that already have cloud accounts and someone who manages them.
  2. Put the accounts in the company’s name. The hosting account, domain and server passwords must belong to the company, registered to a company email, with the owner holding the master credentials. The most common self-hosting failure in small businesses is a server that only the developer or one ex-employee could log in to.
  3. Set up backups, and test restores. Follow the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of the data, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy off-site. Automate the schedule. Then, at least once a quarter, restore a backup to a test machine and check the data. A backup that has never been restored is only a hope.
  4. Control access. Every person gets an individual login — no shared passwords — with a role that limits what they can see. Remove access the day someone leaves. Turn on two-factor authentication for the hosting account.
  5. Keep the server secure and updated. Apply operating system updates, serve the application over HTTPS, close unused ports with a firewall, and keep server admin access to a small number of named people.
  6. Decide who is called when it breaks. Name the person or IT partner responsible for uptime and recovery, and write the recovery steps down where the owner can find them.
  7. Keep an exit plan. Make sure all data can be exported in open formats such as CSV or JSON. Ownership means being able to move, not just being able to stay.

Frequently asked questions

What does self-hosted ERP mean?

A self-hosted ERP is business software installed on a server your company controls, such as an office server, a VPS or a private cloud account, instead of running on the software vendor’s shared cloud. Your company holds the database, the backups and the access rules.

Is self-hosting safer than cloud SaaS?

Not automatically. Self-hosting gives you control over where data lives and who can reach it, but safety depends on how well the server, backups and access are managed. A well-run SaaS can be safer than a neglected office server.

Does self-hosted mean no running costs?

No. You avoid a software subscription, but you still pay for hosting or hardware, power and internet, and someone’s time to look after backups, updates and access. Plan for those costs honestly.

What do I need to run BOOM on my own server?

An on-premise server, a VPS or a private cloud instance running Linux or Windows Server with a standard web stack, plus a named person responsible for backups and access. YNIIDI installs and configures BOOM on your server during deployment and trains your admin on users, permissions and backups.

Can I get all my data out of BOOM?

Yes. BOOM’s admin panel offers a complete JSON backup of customers, orders, products, payments, suppliers and purchases, a restore option, and CSV exports of individual tables. These functions are limited to users with admin roles.
